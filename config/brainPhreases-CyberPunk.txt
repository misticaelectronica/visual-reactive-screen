Residential towers rose from a continuous mass of viaducts, old industrial buildings, service ducts and terraces converted into living spaces.
Their façades were covered in luminous surfaces that changed images and information according to traffic, weather and the profile of the people passing by.
In the central districts, almost every activity was automated, and the environment reacted to the presence of its inhabitants before they had even made a request.
Personal identity allowed people to enter buildings, use transport, access services and communicate with public infrastructure.
Over the years, that dependence had become so normal that it was no longer perceived as a form of political organisation.
A technician responsible for territorial systems began to notice anomalies in the eastern outskirts.
Entire groups of buildings continued to consume energy and water while producing almost no information.
People lived inside them, but they used local networks, autonomous archives and systems that did not communicate with the central infrastructure.
During an inspection, the technician reached one of these districts.
It had grown inside an old industrial complex, in an area where the towers gave way to low structures, abandoned railway lines and large buildings open to the sky.
The warehouses had been connected by walkways, greenhouses and lightweight constructions that followed the shape of the existing buildings.
Vegetation occupied the courtyards and climbed over the metal structures without being treated as decoration.
Water was collected from the roofs, filtered and distributed through an independent network.
Energy came from many different systems, none of them important enough to stop the entire district if it failed.
The machines used to produce components, tools, medicines and materials had been designed so they could be copied and modified.
There was no single platform coordinating those activities.
Information moved through distributed archives connected to other settlements.
A modification developed in one district could appear a few hours later in a community on the other side of the continent.
No one had designed the network as a whole.
It had grown because each group had built only what it needed and had left others free to copy it.
The technician remained in the district longer than expected.
What was there did not resemble the marginal communities described in the city’s reports.
There had been no rejection of technology.
Technology had simply been separated from exclusive ownership.
People owned objects, homes and private spaces, but the infrastructure required for life was considered too important to belong to anyone.
Water, energy, knowledge and productive capacity were distributed in ways that allowed them to be replicated.
The security of the system did not depend on protecting a centre.
It depended on ensuring that no centre was indispensable.
This idea also changed relationships between people.
Anyone controlling a function could gain influence, but it was difficult to preserve that influence for long if others could reproduce the same function elsewhere.
The possibility of leaving a structure limited the power of those who administered it.
The technician understood that this was the real difference between the settlements and the city.The city offered freedom within a system that was almost impossible to leave.
The settlements built systems that could always be left behind.
They were not seeking self-sufficiency.
They continued to exchange materials, knowledge and services with the outside world.
They were seeking something harder to measure: the ability to refuse a relationship without placing their own existence at risk.
Over the following years, the model spread.
Settlements appeared on the outskirts of large cities, in abandoned industrial zones, along the coasts and in spaces left unused by automated infrastructure.
Each community took a different form.
Some were organised around research, others around production, others around cultivation or care.
There was no capital, no common government and no organisation capable of representing them all.
They shared only protocols, knowledge and a few principles of interoperability.
The central city continued to grow and refine its systems.
Its towers became taller, its services more efficient and its algorithms more precise.
For a long time, it regarded the settlements as a peripheral phenomenon.
Then it began to lose something that no economic indicator could describe properly.
It was losing necessity.
More and more people could move through the central system without depending entirely on it.
That was when the institutions understood that they were not witnessing an escape.
They were witnessing the birth of a second society.
There was no government to overthrow in order to stop it.
There was no leader to arrest.
There was not even a precise place where that society could be located.
Its strength lay precisely in the fact that it could be rebuilt wherever there were people willing to treat infrastructure as a common good and power as a dependency that could be made avoidable.
For the first time, the city found itself facing an adversary that did not want to conquer it.
It simply wanted to make it possible to live without it.
