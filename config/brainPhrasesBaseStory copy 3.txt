In a towering future metropolis of glass, steel and permanent night, a silent alarm spreads through the Metropolitan Museum as every screen suddenly turns red. Masterpieces hang in vast white rooms beneath cold security lights, while a faceless intruder hijacks the entire system from afar. His message is simple: pay one million credits, or watch the collection vanish. In the empty galleries, paintings, sculptures and digital relics seem suspended on the edge of execution, as if culture itself were being held hostage by an invisible intelligence.
The blackmailer is eventually identified as Igor McNally, a legendary cyber-criminal hiding inside the decayed layers of the city, where cables hang like roots and neon reflects on wet concrete. He is not a common thief but a man who treats networks like living tissue, moving through them with surgical precision. Yet while the authorities close in on him, another horror is already spreading through the city: a chain of murders targeting celebrated artists. Each crime scene is composed with disturbing elegance, turning the dead into part of a deliberate visual statement.
The press gives the unknown murderer a name: Bauhaus Killer. His victims are not chosen at random. Painters, sculptors, designers, creators of form and meaning are found in environments arranged with almost ritual control, as though the killer wants to reduce art to geometry, purity and annihilation. Bright studios, private galleries and design lofts become stages of death. Every murder looks less like an act of rage than like a cold aesthetic manifesto, where blood, space and objects are arranged with terrifying intention.
The investigation takes a darker turn when it becomes clear that McNally knows something vital about the killer’s identity and method. Locked away but still dangerous, he bargains from a position of strange power: he will help only if his freedom is granted. The detectives are forced into a grim exchange with a man who weaponized the museum, hoping to stop another man who is weaponizing beauty itself. In interrogation rooms lit by pale monitors, the tension becomes almost philosophical: how much corruption can be accepted in order to prevent a greater violence?
The final pursuit unfolds as a race through a city where art, memory and technology have become inseparable. The killer is no longer just a murderer but the embodiment of a terrifying idea: that art can outlive the artist by consuming them, and that destruction itself can be framed as creation. The hunt moves through exhibition halls, electronic archives and sterile architectural spaces where every line feels intentional, every shadow designed. What remains at the end is not only the image of a killer being pursued, but the deeper question haunting the whole story: whether beauty is something preserved by civilization, or something that civilization is always on the verge of destroying.
