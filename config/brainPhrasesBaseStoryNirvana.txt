The Northern Agglomeration stretches beneath a grey winter sky, a dense field of towers, elevated roads, cables and luminous signs. The city is cold, crowded and already worn out by its own technology; rain darkens the concrete while artificial colour survives everywhere.
Jimi Dini stands alone in a high apartment above the city. He is pale, tired and controlled, surrounded by blue monitors, glass surfaces and silent machines. Behind him, large windows reveal the distant glow of the metropolis.
On Jimi’s screens runs Nirvana, the videogame he has created for Okosama Starr. Clean graphics and corporate interfaces contrast with the dark room around him. The product is almost ready for release.
Inside the game, Solo inhabits another city. He looks completely human: middle-aged, heavy-faced, dark-haired, dressed like an ordinary man passing through a dirty urban world. Nothing about him suggests that he is made of code.
A virus enters the program and Solo begins to notice repetition. The same street, the same people and the same violence return around him. His surroundings remain solid, but his expression changes from confusion to suspicion.
Solo dies and the world begins again. He wakes inside familiar rooms with the same furniture, the same light and the same sequence waiting outside. Repetition becomes visible in the architecture itself.
He starts examining doors, walls and faces as if the surface of reality might reveal its machinery. The city around him keeps moving normally while he becomes increasingly still, watching instead of participating.
Solo reaches beyond the program and appears before Jimi through the computer. One man sits in a real room, the other stands inside a constructed one, separated by a screen that suddenly feels too thin.
Jimi watches Solo with disbelief. The artificial character is no longer behaving like a character; his posture, fatigue and attention belong to someone who understands that his world has been built around him.
The request is simple: Nirvana must be erased. Jimi remains seated before the screen while the blue light of the program illuminates his face and the apartment behind him falls into darkness.
The problem leads outside Jimi’s home. The master copy of Nirvana is stored inside the protected systems of Okosama Starr, beyond the reach of a simple local deletion.
Jimi descends into the Northern Agglomeration. The clean silence of his apartment gives way to wet streets, crowded passages, rusted shutters, hanging wires, steam from food stalls and flickering fluorescent tubes.
Technology here is physical and exhausted. Monitors are scratched, terminals are repaired with mismatched parts, cables run openly across walls, and new electronic devices sit beside objects that look decades old.
Above the crowd rises Kālī, enormous on an electronic billboard. Her skin is saturated cobalt blue, her hair black, her ornaments gold, her mouth open with a vivid red tongue extending beneath her teeth.
Her multiple arms spread around the body in ritual gestures while commercial graphics surround her. The goddess dominates a façade stained by rain and pollution, turning sacred imagery into the luminous face of Nirvana.
Pedestrians pass beneath Kālī without looking up. Jimi disappears among them, a small dark figure moving below a blue body several storeys high.
Jimi searches for Joystick in the poorer districts. Streets contract into corridors between damaged buildings, markets spill into doorways, and illegal technology is sold beside food, clothes and medicine.
Joystick emerges from this environment thin, nervous and quick. His face is marked by his missing natural eyes; the artificial replacements give his gaze an uncanny stillness against the restless movement of his body.
His body belongs to the same economy as the machines around him. Eyes can be sold, replaced, upgraded or lost, and the face itself becomes a record of the city’s trade in flesh and technology.
Joystick leads Jimi through workshops and back rooms. Small televisions glow beside exposed circuit boards, metal shelves sag under electronic parts, and improvised clinics hide behind curtains and reinforced doors.
Jimi’s search for Nirvana remains tied to another search: Lisa. Her face appears in memory against quieter spaces, pale skin and dark hair isolated from the noise of the city.
Lisa is absent from the streets but present inside Jimi’s attention. A room, a screen or a gesture can suddenly bring her image forward, as if memory were another layer projected over the city.
The search leads toward Naima, a young hacker who inhabits the same border between human body and machine. She stands in a cramped electronic interior lit by monitors and low industrial lamps.
Naima’s short blue hair immediately separates her from the brown walls and dirty metal around her. Near one eyebrow, a small interface is embedded into the skin, precise and almost elegant.
When Naima connects to a system, the body becomes part of the hardware. Cables approach the face; screens change; her stillness contrasts with the rapid movement of data around her.
Jimi, Joystick and Naima form a visually mismatched group. Jimi is restrained and almost monochrome, Joystick is restless and damaged, Naima sharp and electric, her blue hair echoing the colour of Kālī above the city.
They move together through the Agglomeration. Crowds close around them, vehicles pass through wet streets, advertisements slide across building façades, and every surface seems occupied by information.
Kālī appears again in the distance, blue against grey architecture. The enormous image repeats across the city just as Solo’s world repeats inside the game.
Inside Nirvana, Solo walks through familiar rooms with growing exhaustion. Furniture, light and people return to their assigned positions; only Solo’s awareness has changed.
He touches a wall, watches a stranger approach, studies a doorway before passing through it. Ordinary objects now appear suspicious because he knows that each one exists only as part of a programmed sequence.
Another cycle begins around him. The scene fills with movement while Solo seems detached from it, a real-looking body surrounded by behaviour that has become visibly mechanical to him.
Back in the physical city, Jimi and his companions prepare to penetrate Okosama Starr. Their equipment occupies an ordinary room: cheap tables, exposed wiring, old monitors and improvised connections.
There is no futuristic command centre. The attack on a multinational corporation begins among worn furniture and second-hand machines, with human bodies bent close to glowing screens.
Naima works at the terminals while Joystick watches the data. Jimi sits at the centre of the equipment, cables connecting him to a system far larger than the room containing his body.
The connection opens into a virtual environment. The walls of the room disappear from Jimi’s perception and are replaced by dark artificial spaces, luminous structures and corridors without physical material.
The corporate network becomes a landscape. Information takes the form of distance, passage and obstruction; Jimi moves through data as if crossing rooms inside an invisible building.
Faces from his own life begin to appear. The network uses familiar people and memories as surfaces, surrounding Jimi with images more difficult to pass than any mechanical barrier.
Lisa emerges from the darkness with startling clarity. Her pale face, dark hair and familiar expression occupy the virtual space as if she had stepped back into physical existence.
Jimi approaches the image and the corporate system closes around his attention. The environment becomes less architectural and more intimate, built from remembered faces rather than walls.
Outside the connection, Naima and Joystick remain in the cramped room. Their faces are lit by unstable monitors while Jimi’s body sits almost motionless among cables.
Jimi forces himself deeper into the system. Images dissolve behind him; new spaces assemble ahead; the clean geometry of corporate data alternates with fragments of personal memory.
He reaches the protected layers containing Nirvana. The game is no longer a box, logo or commercial product but a complete artificial environment sustained somewhere inside racks of corporate machinery.
Solo waits inside that environment. He stands in an ordinary street under artificial light, surrounded by buildings and people still performing the lives written for them.
For a moment the two worlds seem to face each other: Jimi inside the corporate network, Solo inside the game, each surrounded by a reality made from information.
Jimi begins the deletion. There is no need for fire or explosion; the visual world around Solo simply starts losing permanence.
Edges soften, spaces disappear, backgrounds fail to return. The artificial city becomes incomplete, as though parts of a painted surface were being removed while Solo still stands inside it.
Solo remains at the centre, a tired human figure against a world that can no longer sustain itself. His ordinary face makes the disappearance around him more unsettling than any monstrous image could.
Then Solo vanishes with Nirvana. The programmed street, its walls, lights and repeating inhabitants disappear with him.
The physical city continues. Rain remains on the pavement, crowds move through markets, screens flicker above damaged buildings, and the machinery of the Northern Agglomeration does not acknowledge what has happened.
High above those streets, Kālī still burns in blue and gold, her red tongue extended over the city. Beneath her, human bodies, artificial eyes, memories and machines continue moving through the same exhausted light.
